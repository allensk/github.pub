Image basics: compression, raster and distortion

When working with pictures, a few questions come up often:

  • Why are some picture files big and others small?
  • Why does a picture get blurry when enlarged?
  • Why do some logos stay sharp when enlarged?

Understanding a few basic concepts explains all of it.

At a glance

Concept One sentence
Lossless compression Decompresses back to the original picture, like PNG
Lossy compression Trades some detail for a smaller file, like JPG
Raster image Made of pixels, scaling distorts it
Vector image Made of math descriptions, scaling never distorts

I. Lossless and lossy compression

Lossless compression

Loses no original data, decompressing restores the complete original picture, for example PNG.

Pros Cons
Keeps the original quality, compression does not lose quality The file is usually bigger
Supports transparent backgrounds —

Lossy compression

Drops part of the image information to get a smaller file, for example JPEG (JPG).

Pros Cons
The file is usually smaller Compression may lose quality
Especially good for photos and scenery Repeated editing and saving lowers quality further

In short:

  • PNG: keeps all the details, the file may be bigger.
  • JPEG: trades some detail for a smaller file.

II. Raster and vector images

Raster images (Raster Graphics)

A raster image is made of pixels (Pixel), each pixel records the color of its position.

Take a 1920 × 1080 picture:

  • 1920 pixels across
  • 1080 pixels down
  • all the pixels together form the complete image

Think of it as a picture assembled from many small colored blocks.

Common formats: JPG, PNG, WebP, BMP.

Vector images (Vector Graphics)

A vector image does not record the color of each pixel, it describes shapes with math, for example lines, curves, shapes and colors, and recomputes them when displaying.

Pros Cons
Can be freely enlarged and reduced Not good for complex details like photos
Scaling never causes pixel blur Needs dedicated software to create
Perfect for logos, icons, lines and simple shapes —

Common format: SVG.

III. Why scaling a raster image distorts it

A raster image has a limited number of pixels; when the size changes, the pixels must be reworked.

Shrinking

For example from 512 × 512 down to 256 × 256: the pixels go from 262,144 to 65,536.

The system has to merge or recompute pixels, some original detail is lost, so a shrunken picture can never be restored to the original.

Enlarging

For example from 3 × 3 up to 4 × 4: the original has 9 pixels, the enlarged picture needs 16.

The new pixels have no original data, the system can only estimate their colors from the neighbors, so the picture may become blurry or jagged.

In short:

  • Shrinking: fewer pixels, some detail is lost.
  • Enlarging: more pixels, the new detail can only be estimated.

IV. What anti-aliasing can do

Anti-aliasing smooths the color transition between edge pixels and reduces the jagged feel of lines and shapes.

For example, enlarging a slanted line by copying pixels shows clear stair steps; with anti-aliasing the edge looks smoother.

Note: anti-aliasing only improves the visual effect, it cannot restore the original detail that was lost.

V. How this relates to RectIcon

  • RectIcon exports PNG, a lossless format, good for screenshots, icons and transparent pictures, quality does not degrade with reuse.
  • After a capture, enlarging or shrinking the picture still distorts a raster image — when you need several sizes, capture again at the target size instead of enlarging a small picture.
  • The built-in editor’s crop, zoom and transparency are pixel based; use the Undo menu to restore after an operation.

VI. Summary

Compare Raster Vector
Representation Pixels Math described shapes
Enlarging May blur and distort Scaling never distorts
Shrinking May lose detail Shapes are recomputed
Good for Photos, scenery, complex images Logos, icons, line graphics
Common formats JPG, PNG, WebP SVG

Remember these for daily use:

  • PNG: lossless, good for screenshots, transparent pictures and icons.
  • JPEG: lossy, good for photos, usually smaller files.
  • Raster: enlarging or shrinking can hurt quality.
  • Vector: scales freely, good for logos and icons.
  • Anti-aliasing: makes edges smoother, but cannot recover lost detail.
